# Credit Analysis

Framework from _The Credit Investor's Handbook: Leveraged Loans, High Yield, and Distressed Debt_ by Michael Gatto (Wiley, 2024).

---

## Core Concept

A creditor earns a fixed return and does not share upside, so the focus is downside protection. Every credit decision answers two questions: the probability of default, and the severity of loss (loss given default, whose inverse is recovery). The framework subsumes the traditional 5 Cs. The process is iterative, not linear.

```text
Step 1  Sources and Uses            (originations only)
Step 2  Qualitative Analysis
Step 3  Financial Statement Analysis  (profitability, cash flow and liquidity, capital structure)
Step 4  Forecasting
Step 5  Corporate Valuation
Step 6  Structuring and Documentation
Step 7  Investment Recommendation and Credit Committee Memo
```

The framework values the whole enterprise, so it also supports an equity view when paired with the capital structure.

---

## Step 1: Sources and Uses (originations only)

Answer two questions: why is the company seeking financing, and what are the proposed sources?

Reasons to seek financing: fund a leveraged buyout (LBO); fund seasonal inventory; fund growth (capital expenditure, or capex); fund an acquisition; fund shareholder initiatives (repurchase, dividend recapitalization); fund operating losses; refinance maturing debt.

Instruments from safest to riskiest: secured revolver or asset-based loan (ABL: first lien on working capital, borrowing base capped near 90% of eligible receivables and 85% of inventory liquidation value); pari passu revolver; secured term loans (first lien, second lien, unitranche); senior unsecured high yield bonds; subordinated mezzanine debt. The sources and uses table opens a credit committee memo.

A sponsor financing an LBO chooses between the broadly syndicated market (cheaper, slower, less certain, exposed to flex and hung deals) and private credit (costlier, faster, more certain).

---

## Step 2: Qualitative Analysis

Identify three to five material risks, decide whether each is analyzable, and carry them into Step 4.

Eight common risks: secularly declining industry; adverse competitive dynamics; cyclicality; government regulation; customer concentration; product concentration; fad/fashion; commodity (selling price or cost structure).

For each, quantify the exposure, test it against peers, and model a downside case. For concentration, judge the customer's creditworthiness, the switching cost, and the impact of loss. For regulation, use outside counsel to size the change.

Judge the strategy (is it clear and executable? use Porter's generic strategies or SWOT if useful), management (track record, guidance versus actuals, ownership and insider transactions, compensation metrics), and ESG fit (firm policy and investor reaction).

## Step 3a: Profitability (six sub-steps)

1. Adjust reported income for nonrecurring items (restructuring, impairments, merger costs, asset sale gains or losses, litigation), using footnotes and management discussion and analysis (MD&A). Beware ordinary costs labeled nonrecurring.
2. Build a summary adjusted income statement: three to five years for a noncyclical business, a full cycle including a recession for a cyclical one.
3. Analyze earnings quality.
4. Build adjusted statements for the peer group.
5. Draw conclusions versus expectations and peers.
6. List follow-ups in priority order.

Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization (EBITDA) is the core credit metric and the basis for covenants. Build last twelve months (LTM) as last fiscal year plus year-to-date, less the prior-year year-to-date.

Sales red flags: dealer dumping (check receivable days versus peers); orders lagging shipments (book-to-bill = new orders / sales, below 1.0 overstates growth); foreign exchange; acquisitions masking organic decline.

Expense red flags: absorption costing (over-production inflates margin while inventory days rise; Cisco's inventory days went from the low 40s to 78 before a $2.4 billion write-down); differing cost allocation between cost of goods sold and selling, general and administrative expense (compare EBITDA margins); cuts to research and development or advertising; understated bad debt (track the allowance for doubtful accounts); improper capitalization of operating expense. Also watch "big bath" accounting, where charges are overstated in a loss year to flatter future profit. For private originations, a third-party quality of earnings report is standard.

## Step 3b: Cash Flow and Liquidity (ten sub-steps)

Ignore the current ratio; it is not a liquidity measure. Steps: static liquidity (cash plus revolver availability); five-year debt maturity schedule; free cash flow; cash cycle and working capital; ability to raise capital and refinance; trade support; capex flexibility; other levers; conclusions; follow-ups.

```text
Static liquidity = excess cash + committed revolver availability
FCF = adjusted EBITDA - capex - cash interest - cash taxes +/- change in working capital
Inventory days  = 365 x average inventory / COGS
Receivable days = 365 x average receivables / sales
Payable days    = 365 x average payables / COGS
Cash cycle      = inventory days + receivable days - payable days
```

A sustainable rise in payable days helps liquidity. Stretching payables is temporary and can pull the entire accounts payable balance due at once if vendors tighten terms or demand cash on delivery. Other levers: sell non-core assets, securitize receivables, raise the ABL borrowing base or add a first-in, last-out (FILO) tranche, sell a division. Separate maintenance capex (not deferrable) from growth capex (deferrable).

---

## Step 3c: Capital Structure (six sub-steps)

Lay out the capital structure table; calculate ratios; adjust for off-balance-sheet obligations; review current, historical, and trajectory ratings; conclude; list follow-ups. Track the rating trajectory: a downgrade from investment grade to high yield (a "fallen angel") forces index-constrained holders to sell.

```text
Leverage = debt / adjusted EBITDA
Loan-to-value (LTV) = debt / enterprise value
Coverage = (adjusted EBITDA - capex) / interest expense
Total enterprise value (TEV) = debt - cash + equity market capitalization
```

Coverage above 2.0x is healthy and below 1.0x signals distress; a 90% LTV means enterprise value can fall only 10% before creditors are impaired. Compute leverage on a face and a market basis; when debt trades below par, use senior debt at face and the analyzed layer at market. Priority: first lien, second lien, senior unsecured, subordinated, preferred, common.

Debt-like obligations: capital leases as secured debt; operating leases by adding eight times rent to debt and one times rent to EBITDA, or by adding the disclosed lease liability and rent (preferred when the two differ); underfunded defined benefit pensions (projected benefit obligation minus plan assets) added to debt with pension expense added to EBITDA; other contingent liabilities (environmental, litigation, guarantees).

## Step 4: Forecasting

Use at least three cases: management or consensus, base, and downside. A good base case is a coin toss; being too conservative is nearly as bad as being too aggressive. Downside cases must carry the Step 2 risks.

Forecast the statements in sequence: input history; build an assumption page; forecast the income statement; forecast the balance sheet excluding cash and debt; forecast cash flow and link it to the balance sheet, driving working capital off the days ratios and letting the revolver be the plug; run the cases; summarize ratios; assign probabilities. Because interest depends on average debt outstanding, cash flow and the balance sheet are circular, so iterate. No hard-coded projection cells.

---

## Step 5: Corporate Valuation

Use more than one method: comparable companies, precedent transactions, discounted cash flow, and liquidation analysis (for deeply distressed companies whose value is in remaining assets). The purpose in credit is to measure the cushion before each debt layer is impaired.

---

## Step 6: Structuring and Documentation

Review: amount, maturity, amortization and excess cash flow (ECF) sweep (25% to 75%), coupon, upfront fees or original issue discount, call protection, change of control put, subordination, collateral, covenants, amendments.

Loans are usually floating (historically LIBOR, now SOFR, often with a floor) and bonds fixed; watch pricing grids, step-ups, and payment in kind (PIK) toggles. Call protection runs from make-whole (strongest), to hard call (loans at 103/102/101; bonds at par plus half the coupon after the first half of life), to soft call, to none.

Loans carry maintenance covenants (tested regularly; a breach permits acceleration but usually leads to a waiver); bonds carry incurrence covenants (tested only on a specific action). Negative covenants are full of baskets and carve-outs.

Covenant EBITDA add-backs (sponsor fees, stock compensation, projected cost savings and synergies) are more permissive than analyst adjustments and can hide distress (for example, Realogy). Negotiate them for new issues; map them for secondary trades.

Set financial covenants with a 15% to 30% cushion to management's EBITDA, starting from the management case. Amendments need a majority by par, except sacred rights at 100% (maturity, amortization, coupon, commitment increase, release of substantially all collateral, pro rata sharing, disproportionate adverse changes).

---

## Step 7: Investment Recommendation and Credit Committee Memo

A concise memo: an up-front recommendation with relative value, the thesis, significant risks with mitigants, and enough analysis to debate. Firms use a short teaser and a full credit committee memo. Relative value answers whether this is a good investment against everything else the firm can invest in.

Trading vocabulary: prices are a percentage of par, so 84 means 84 cents on the dollar. Yield to maturity is the return if the debt is held and repaid. Spread is the yield over the risk-free benchmark. A bond is "cheap" when its spread overcompensates for the risk and "rich" when it does not. For every 1% move in rates or spread, price moves roughly 1% the opposite way per year of duration; longer maturity and lower coupon mean higher duration. Measure expected return with internal rate of return and multiple of money, and weigh it against the loss scenario, not just the upside.

## Equity Through a Creditor's Lens

Enterprise value less net debt is equity, so the creditor's enterprise value view yields an equity view.

| Dimension | Creditor | Equity holder |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Payoff | Fixed coupon and principal; no upside | Residual; all upside after creditors |
| Focus | Probability of default and loss severity | Size and probability of the upside |
| Failure | Partial or full loss, often recoverable | Total wipeout possible |

A change that excites equity can be reckless for credit. JCPenney's pricing overhaul had real equity upside, but creditors were already at par, so they had no upside and large downside.

Leverage lifts return on equity when return on assets exceeds the after-tax cost of debt, but rising debt raises the cost of debt and the risk of distress. Stable cash flows support more debt; volatile cash flows support less.

The capital structure shows how much enterprise value must exist before each layer is whole and before equity gets anything. In a downside, equity can go to zero while creditors are still paid. Debt is capped near par by call protection; equity is uncapped, so a short debt position has bounded downside and a short equity position does not.

## Gotchas

- The seven steps are iterative; revisit earlier steps when later analysis changes the picture.
- Use one consistent adjusted EBITDA. Covenant definitions are more permissive and can hide deterioration.
- Reject the current ratio. Use static liquidity, the maturity schedule, free cash flow, and the cash cycle.
- Face and market leverage diverge only when debt trades below par.
- Use the balance-sheet operating lease liability method over the 8x rent rule when they diverge.
- The cash drain from stretched payables is the whole accounts payable balance, not the change in payable days.
- Equity is the residual after net debt. Small enterprise value declines can wipe equity out while creditors stay whole.
